# MiniProject3 — Validation Reflection

**Greyson Wright (`gwrigh30`)**  
**Partner:** Hari Naicker (`hnaicker`)

Going through Hari's projects, I noticed pretty quickly that our biggest differences were in how we described activity over time. Five of the activity-pattern classifications didn't match. What made it interesting was that his notebook sometimes supported the answer I had picked, even though the exported CSV said something else. For example, he described siibra-explorer as declining and deeptime as U-shaped, but the CSV called both irregular. We talked about how his classification function and his own interpretation of the graphs didn't always line up, and we went with the patterns that best matched the overall trends.

The `Currentstatus` differences were easier to settle because the assignment gives us an actual cutoff date. We were supposed to judge activity as of September 30, 2025, not based on whatever the repositories have done since. VineCopula and phuzzy both had their last commits before April 1, 2025, and AiiDA Tutorials didn't get another commit until October 2. That made all three Inactive under the assignment's definition. On the other hand, vdt and deeptime had April commits, so they still counted as Active. It's a good example of how the exact question matters more than just looking at whether a repository seems alive today.

The recovery part was less clear-cut. In pyAudioAnalysis, a contributor submitted dependency changes and the maintainer merged them after the gap. deeptime had a new developer come in and start doing actual implementation work, while VineCopula returned to bug fixes and maintenance. Those were fairly good signs that work had picked up again. But deforest-watch was different: it had one housekeeping commit after a long gap, and that didn't really look like development starting back up. When Hari reviewed my projects, we had a similar discussion about shellphish/driller. I originally called its single compatibility commit a partial recovery, but we agreed that No made more sense if we're talking about meaningful renewed development. CatBoost also came up because it was being worked on continuously, but its commit volume varied too much for us to call the pattern steady.

The messiest part was `RecentThemes`. Hari's original stats CSV didn't have those values filled in, so I went through the recent commits and classified them myself. After our call, he updated his file. Eight of his ten values matched what I'd found, and we checked the remaining two over Discord. We agreed on `Other:Documentation updates` for statistics-coded and `Bug fixes:Other` for phuzzy. I left the original blanks in the pre-discussion data because otherwise it would look like both of us had those answers available from the beginning. That also explains why the before-discussion agreement for this field shows 0%: the labels were missing, not necessarily wrong.

For me, the main takeaway was that the commit data and the classification table aren't automatically the same thing. Dates can be read against the wrong time window, a script can label a trend differently from the graph, and a generic commit message doesn't always tell you what actually changed. Talking through specific examples helped us get more consistent answers, but there are still things the Git history alone can't tell us, especially why someone stopped contributing in the first place.
